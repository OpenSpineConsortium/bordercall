# Amonoo-Kuofi 1982: Lumbar interpedicular distances in adult Nigerians

Source: thread "Reread every paper for clinical detail", project symphony_4, 2026-10-28.

Citation as printed: H. S. Amonoo-Kuofi. Maximum and minimum lumbar interpedicular distances in normal adult Nigerians. J. Anat. (1982), 135, 2, pp. 225-233 (with 6 figures; accepted 29 September 1981). Department of Anatomy, Faculty of Health Sciences, University of Ilorin, Nigeria.

Nine PDF pages = journal pp. 225-233 (PDF 1 = p. 225 ... PDF 9 = p. 233). Every value below was read from the page images (Fig. 4, 5 and 6 also from 300 dpi renders). ## What the paper is

- Design: cross-sectional radiographic norm study, one observer, no repeat measurement. The films were selected from casualty attendances (Inference: retrospectively; the paper says only that radiographs "were selected"). Aim stated as two questions: are there significant regional or ethnic differences in lumbar canal size, and what are the maximum and minimum limits of normal lumbar canal width in Nigerians, p. 225 (PDF 1).
- Population: 290 living adult Nigerians, 150 men and 140 women, aged 20 to 45 years, who came to the casualty (emergency) department of the University of Ilorin Teaching Hospital for suspected recent accidental injury to the lumbar spine and in whom no bony injury was found; films of anyone who had come to hospital with backache were excluded, p. 225 (PDF 1). Ethnic group within Nigeria is not stated. Inference: the sample is "normal" only in the sense of no fracture and no back-pain attendance; no clinical examination for stenosis is reported.
- Films: plain anteroposterior (AP) lumbar radiographs, one per patient (290 films), screened for readability and first interpreted by a diagnostic radiologist; all taken at an anode-film distance of 100 cm, pp. 225-226 (PDF 1-2). How many films failed the readability screen is not stated.
- Instrument: vernier calipers on the film, read to the nearest 0.1 mm, all by the author, p. 226 (PDF 2). No correction for radiographic magnification.
- Levels: L1 to L5, both sexes separately (Tables 1-4). Nothing above L1 or at the sacrum.
- How levels were identified and counted: not stated. Transitional vertebrae, six-lumbar or four-lumbar spines are not mentioned, neither excluded nor handled. Per-level n is not given; Inference: all 290 films at every level.
- Statistics: mean, SD and coefficient of variation per level and sex (Tables 1, 3); "95% tolerance ranges" by the formula in Bradford Hill (1977), described as the high and low limits holding the central 95% of normals (Table 2), p. 228 (PDF 4). No significance tests, no correlation coefficients, no regression on stature, age or weight.
- Background the author gives: plain radiographs, myelography, epidural venography, CT and diagnostic ultrasound had all been used to size the lumbar canal, and plain films were judged of great value in diagnosing lumbar stenosis (citing Chynn et al. 1978), p. 225 (PDF 1). Earlier normal values from plain films: Hinck et al. (1966) in white Americans and Eisenstein (1977) in black and white South Africans, with marked differences between the two, and Eisenstein reporting the black ("negroid") canal marginally less capacious than the white ("caucasoid") one, p. 225 (PDF 1).

## Anomalies, numbering and transitional segments

- The paper gives no anomaly prevalence of any kind: no LSTV, sacralisation, lumbarisation, L6, T13, rib counts, block vertebrae or spina bifida. Numbering method not stated.
- Trisomy 21: citing Weir and Abrahams (1978, a radiological anatomy atlas), the author notes that in "mongolism" the interpedicular distances can decrease from above downwards instead of rising, p. 232 (PDF 8). No prevalence, no values.
- Any anomaly: a carefully measured interpedicular distance outside the Table 2 limits should raise suspicion of pathology or anomaly, while some abnormal values can still fall inside the limits, p. 228 (PDF 4).
- Inference: an unrecognised LSTV would shift the labels; because the interpedicular distance climbs most steeply from L4 to L5 (Table 1), a mislabelled L5 or L6 would be read against the wrong norm.

## Measures, each as a recipe

### Interpedicular distance (IPD, "A" in Fig. 1)

- Definition: the shortest distance between the medial surfaces of the two pedicles of one vertebra, measured on the AP film, p. 226 (PDF 2), Fig. 1 (arrow A drawn between the medial borders of the pedicle ovals of a mid-lumbar vertebra, at the height of the pedicles in the upper part of the body).
- What "maximum and minimum" means here (special attention): the title's maximum and minimum are NOT two measurement positions along the pedicle. The paper takes one measurement per vertebra, the shortest medial-to-medial gap; "maximum and minimum" are the upper and lower normal limits of that one measure. The introduction's second question asks for the maximum and minimum limits of normal canal width in Nigerians, p. 225 (PDF 1); Table 2 gives them as 95% tolerance ranges, and the Fig. 4 caption calls them "Maximum and minimum limits (95% tolerance ranges)", p. 229 (PDF 5). The title follows the usage of Hinck, Clark and Hopkins (1966), whose title reads "Normal interpediculate distances (minimum and maximum)", reference list p. 233 (PDF 9); Inference from memory: Hinck's minimum and maximum are likewise normal limits, not two sites. By contrast the author says Eisenstein (1977) took the interpedicular distance as the greatest distance between the pedicles, and treats that definition as one that should give larger values than his own, p. 232 (PDF 8). Inference: on an AP film each pedicle ring is convex medially, so the shortest gap falls at the most medial bulge of the two rings, near mid pedicle height; where Eisenstein's "greatest distance" was taken is not said here, so the two definitions are not interchangeable.
- Projection: AP radiograph only. Lateral films are not used.
- Values, Table 1, p. 228 (PDF 4), mean (SD, CV%), mm on the film, 150 men and 140 women:
  - Men: L1 22.6 (1.9, 8.4); L2 22.7 (1.7, 7.5); L3 24.5 (1.5, 6.1); L4 26.0 (1.7, 6.5); L5 28.7 (2.3, 8.0).
  - Women: L1 21.3 (2.0, 9.4); L2 22.5 (2.0, 8.9); L3 23.7 (1.8, 7.6); L4 25.4 (1.6, 6.3); L5 28.4 (2.0, 7.0).
- Normal limits, Table 2, p. 229 (PDF 5), 95% tolerance range, mm:
  - Men: L1 18.8-26.4; L2 19.3-26.1; L3 21.5-27.5; L4 22.6-29.4; L5 24.1-33.3.
  - Women: L1 17.3-25.3; L2 18.5-26.5; L3 20.1-27.3; L4 22.2-28.6; L5 24.4-32.4.
  - Inference (my arithmetic): every limit equals the Table 1 mean +/- 2.0 SD exactly, so the "tolerance range" is a plain 2 SD band, not a tolerance interval with a sample-size factor.
- From a 3D CT instance mask: Inference (the paper is radiographic). Put the instance in a vertebra frame (x left-right, y antero-posterior, z cranio-caudal, from the body's end-plate normal and the symmetry plane). Isolate the two pedicles from the posterior-element subregion (the bars joining the back of the body to the articular pillars; where the segmenter gives only a posterior-arch label, the pedicles are its anterior part between the body and the articular processes). Project each pedicle along y onto the coronal plane, as the AP beam does. For each z inside the pedicles' cranio-caudal extent, the gap is the left-right distance from the medial edge of the left silhouette to the medial edge of the right silhouette; IPD is the minimum of that gap over z. This approximates the left-right width of the bony canal at its narrowest between the pedicles (the canal hole of the instance in axial slices at pedicle height is a cross-check). Values from CT are true size; multiply by the AP film magnification, or compare ratios, before using Tables 1-2.
- From a radiograph alone: AP lumbar film or AP fluoroscopy; the medial cortical borders of the pedicle ovals. Values carry the film magnification of a 100 cm anode-film distance, p. 226 (PDF 2). Inference: magnification M = source-to-image distance / (source-to-image distance - object-to-image distance); with the canal a few cm in front of the film in a supine AP view, M is of the order of 1.05-1.15, and it differs again on fluoroscopy. A lateral film cannot give the IPD (Inference; the paper uses only AP). Inference: axial rotation of the patient and the obliquity of the beam to L5 in lordosis distort the pedicle rings; the paper says nothing on either.
- Across levels: rises from L1 to L5 in both sexes, p. 227 (PDF 3), Fig. 3. Men are almost flat from L1 to L2 (22.6 to 22.7), then rise 1.8, 1.5 and 2.7 mm; women rise 1.2, 1.2, 1.7 and 3.0 mm. Smallest at L1, largest at L5; the L4 to L5 step is the largest in both sexes. Inference: between-person standardised separation of adjacent levels (difference / mean of the two SDs) is about 0.06 (L1-L2), 1.1 (L2-L3), 0.9 (L3-L4), 1.3 (L4-L5) in men and 0.6, 0.6, 1.0, 1.7 in women: IPD cannot tell L1 from L2 in men, and separates L4 from L5 best.
- Sex, age, size, population: men wider at every level but by only 0.2-1.3 mm (L1 1.3, L2 0.2, L3 0.8, L4 0.6, L5 0.3; Table 1), p. 227 (PDF 3). Women more variable at L1-L3, which the author puts down, tentatively, to greater differences in general body size among women; SDs said to be alike at L4; L5 called the most variable level in both sexes, men more so, p. 228 (PDF 4) (see Gaps for the women). Age: not studied (20-45 only); the author cites Hinck et al. (1966) that the IPD increases with age, is higher in men, and is distinctly narrower before age 19, p. 232 (PDF 8). Size: the IPD keeps a constant ratio to vertebral body width (below), p. 231 (PDF 7). Population: see the comparison in "Level by level" (Fig. 5).
- Error: no intra- or inter-observer data, no repeat; caliper reading to 0.1 mm; film magnification uncorrected; biological SD 1.5-2.3 mm, CV 6.1-9.4% (Table 1).

### Transverse diameter of the vertebral body at the waist (body width, "B" in Fig. 1)

- Definition: the width (transverse diameter) of the vertebral body measured at the narrowest part of the waist of the vertebra, on the AP film, p. 226 (PDF 2), Fig. 1. The author uses it as an index of physique, p. 231 (PDF 7).
- Values, Table 4, p. 231 (PDF 7), mean only (no SD), mm on the film: men L1 41.3, L2 42.9, L3 45.8, L4 49.6, L5 52.8; women L1 37.5, L2 39.7, L3 42.5, L4 45.7, L5 50.5.
- From a 3D CT instance mask: Inference. Take the body subregion; for each axial slice between the end-plates, take the left-right extent of the body silhouette projected along y (the full left-right span at that height, as the AP film shows it); B is the minimum of these over the mid-body, leaving out the flared end-plate rims (for example the top and bottom 15% of body height). Inference: this is not Panjabi's end-plate width, which is taken at the end-plates and should be larger.
- From a radiograph alone: AP film; narrowest silhouette width between the end-plate flares. Magnified like the IPD, though in a supine AP view the body's lateral margins lie further from the film than the pedicles, so slightly more (Inference).
- Across levels: rises steadily L1 to L5, by 11.5 mm in men and 13.0 mm in women; steps of 1.6, 2.9, 3.8, 3.2 mm (men) and 2.2, 2.8, 3.2, 4.8 mm (women), Table 4, p. 231 (PDF 7). The L1 to L2 step is the smallest in both sexes.
- Sex, age, size, population: men 2.3-3.9 mm wider at each level (Table 4). No age or population data.
- Error: none reported; no SD given.

### Canal to body ratio (A/B)

- Definition: IPD divided by the waist width of the same vertebra, computed for each level, p. 226 (PDF 2).
- Values: printed as 0.6 at every level in both sexes, Table 4, p. 231 (PDF 7); "always" 0.6 in normal Nigerians, p. 232 (PDF 8). Inference (my arithmetic from the tabulated means): A/B is 0.547, 0.529, 0.535, 0.524, 0.544 in men (L1-L5) and 0.568, 0.567, 0.558, 0.556, 0.562 in women; the men's values round to 0.5 at one decimal, so the printed 0.6 overstates them; women's ratio is about 0.03 higher than men's.
- From a 3D CT instance mask: the ratio of the two recipes above. Inference: on CT both are true size, so the ratio needs no magnification; on a supine AP film the pedicles lie nearer the film than the body's lateral margins, so the film ratio is a few percent lower than the true ratio.
- From a radiograph alone: AP film or AP fluoroscopy. The author's point is that the ratio removes the need to know the X-ray magnification factor and the person's build, so any AP lumbar film can be used, p. 231 (PDF 7).
- Across levels: flat; it carries no level information, only a canal-size-for-body-size signal (Inference from Table 4).
- Sex, age, size, population: same 0.6 in both sexes as printed (see Inference above). The author argues that if physique differs between groups (men and women, or Hinck's white Americans and his Nigerians), the IPD can be assumed to differ in proportion, p. 231 (PDF 7); he did not measure body width in Hinck's sample (see Gaps).
- Error: none reported; a ratio of two single-observer caliper readings.

### Pedicle index (C x D, Fig. 2)

- Definition: on the same 290 AP films, the right pedicle measured in two mutually perpendicular directions, C its transverse diameter and D its cephalocaudal diameter; the index is the product C x D, pp. 226-227 (PDF 2-3), Fig. 2. Unit not printed. Inference: mm squared on the film (magnification enters squared). The individual C and D values are not reported. Fig. 2 draws C and D on the pedicle ring to the viewer's right of the spinous process, with no side marker visible (Inference: in the usual display that ring is the patient's left, so either the print is reversed or the figure only illustrates the method).
- Rationale: the bony canal depends on pedicles, laminae and articular processes, and very thick pedicles could in theory encroach on the canal, so the pedicle index was measured to relate pedicle size to IPD, p. 230 (PDF 6).
- Values, Table 3, p. 230 (PDF 6), mean (SD, CV%):
  - Men: L1 173.1 (34.5, 19.9); L2 175.6 (32.6, 18.6); L3 190.0 (34.4, 18.1); L4 207.7 (36.0, 17.3); L5 234.6 (47.6, 20.3).
  - Women: L1 141.6 (23.3, 16.5); L2 147.3 (30.4, 20.6); L3 171.0 (30.6, 17.9); L4 183.6 (23.9, 13.0); L5 199.1 (29.9, 15.0).
- From a 3D CT instance mask: Inference. Project the right pedicle along y onto the coronal plane; C = left-right extent and D = cranio-caudal extent of its silhouette; index = C x D. Caveat (Inference): lumbar pedicles converge as they run forward, increasingly toward L5, so any along-the-beam projection, the film's ring or a mask silhouette, is wider than the true pedicle width taken perpendicular to the pedicle axis at its isthmus (Panjabi's width); where the mask draws the pedicle's boundary with the body and the lamina also changes C and D.
- From a radiograph alone: AP film; the pedicle ring's width and height. C is not available from a lateral film; D (pedicle height) is visible on a lateral film (Inference; the paper does not use lateral films).
- Across levels: rises from L1 to L5 in both sexes, men higher at every level, Fig. 6a, p. 231 (PDF 7), Table 3, p. 230 (PDF 6). L1 and L2 nearly equal in men (173.1, 175.6). Inference: adjacent-level standardised separation 0.07, 0.43, 0.50, 0.64 (men) and 0.21, 0.78, 0.46, 0.57 (women): a weak level marker on its own.
- Sex, age, size, population: men larger by 19.0-35.5 units (Table 3). Men have the larger SD at every level and the author says men vary more, p. 230 (PDF 6), though by CV women vary more at L2 (20.6 vs 18.6). The author argues pedicle thickness tracks the muscle bulk the region carries and therefore the build of the person, p. 231 (PDF 7).
- Relation to canal width: pedicle-index CVs (13.0-20.6%) are far above IPD CVs (6.1-9.4%); the author reads this as pedicle size varying without encroaching on the normal canal, the canal widening in proportion with the pedicle index from L1 to L5, Fig. 6b, p. 231 (PDF 7), discussed p. 230 (PDF 6). Inference from Fig. 6b: the women's line lies left of the men's, so at a given IPD women sit at a smaller pedicle index than men.
- Error: none reported; one side only (right); product of two caliper readings, so relative errors add.

## Level by level and at the junctions

The paper covers L1 to L5 only; it says nothing of T12/L1, L5/S1 or any other junction.

- L1: smallest IPD (men 22.6, women 21.3). Largest sex difference in IPD (1.3 mm), larger than the text's claim (see Gaps). Women more variable. Smallest body width (41.3 / 37.5) and pedicle index (173.1 / 141.6). The only level where white South African women (Eisenstein) are wider than Nigerian women, p. 229 (PDF 5).
- L2: men's IPD only 0.1 mm above L1 (22.7), women's 1.2 mm above L1 (22.5); white South African men wider than Nigerian men at L1 and L2, p. 229 (PDF 5).
- L3: IPD 24.5 / 23.7; Nigerian men wider than white South African men from L3 down, p. 229 (PDF 5); Nigerian women wider from L2 down (Fig. 5, p. 230 (PDF 6)).
- L4: IPD 26.0 / 25.4; SDs of the two sexes said to be alike, p. 228 (PDF 4).
- L5: widest canal (28.7 / 28.4) and widest tolerance range in men (24.1-33.3; women 24.4-32.4, the same 8.0 mm width as at L1 and L2). Called the most variable level in both sexes, men more so; the author puts it down to the larger distance at this level and the men's excess to a few men having smaller distances, p. 228 (PDF 4). Largest pedicle index (234.6 / 199.1), largest body width (52.8 / 50.5). Largest step from L4 (2.7 / 3.0 mm).
- Comparison by population, Fig. 5, p. 230 (PDF 6), discussed pp. 229-230 (PDF 5-6). Values are my readings of the graph from a 300 dpi render (approximate, about +/- 0.2 mm), not printed numbers:
  - Men: Hinck et al. (1966), white Americans, L1-L5 about 26.0, 26.6, 26.9, 27.6, 30.7; this study 22.6, 22.7, 24.5, 26.0, 28.7; Eisenstein (1977) caucasoid about 23.1, 24.0, 23.0, 24.0, 26.0; Eisenstein Zulu about 21.1, 22.1, 22.0, 23.0, 26.0 (the two Eisenstein lines meet at L5).
  - Women: Hinck about 24.4, 25.0, 25.5, 26.5, 29.1; this study 21.3, 22.5, 23.7, 25.4, 28.4; Eisenstein caucasoid about 22.1, 22.1, 23.1, 23.1, 24.8; Zulu about 20.1, 21.1, 21.1, 22.1, 24.1.
  - The author's reading: a steady rise from L1 to L5 in all groups; white Americans widest, Zulus narrowest; women below men in every group; Hinck's means fall just inside the Nigerian upper limits, white South African means just inside the Nigerian lower limits, and the Zulu means clearly below the Nigerian lower limits, pp. 229-230 (PDF 5-6). Several of these statements do not match the plotted values (see Gaps).

## Measurement error and reliability

- None measured: one observer (the author), no repeat, no second reader, no phantom, p. 226 (PDF 2).
- Resolution: vernier caliper read to 0.1 mm on film, p. 226 (PDF 2).
- Magnification: anode-film distance fixed at 100 cm for all films, but no correction applied, p. 226 (PDF 2); every mm value is a film value.
- Readability screen and first interpretation by a diagnostic radiologist before measuring, pp. 225-226 (PDF 1-2).
- The author asserts that the pedicles give visible landmarks on AP films so the IPD "can be measured accurately", p. 232 (PDF 8), without data.
- Biological spread: IPD SD 1.5-2.3 mm (CV 6.1-9.4%), Table 1; pedicle index SD 23.3-47.6 (CV 13.0-20.6%), Table 3; body width without SD, Table 4.

## Clinical pearls, indications and pitfalls

- Indication: lumbar spinal stenosis, developmental or acquired; narrowing of the bony ring can compress the cauda equina roots and cause low back pain (citing Sarpyener 1945, Schlesinger and Taveras 1953, Verbiest 1954, 1955, 1977), so canal width is a useful aid in diagnosing the lumbar stenosis syndrome, p. 232 (PDF 8). Plain films are of great value in diagnosing lumbar canal stenosis (citing Chynn et al. 1978), p. 225 (PDF 1).
- Why imaging: most back pain arises from a limited part of the spine (citing Kellgren 1977), and most spinal structures cannot be examined physically, so ancillary methods are needed, p. 232 (PDF 8).
- Inference from the reference title only: the IPD's first use (Elsberg and Dyke 1934, p. 233 (PDF 9)) was localising spinal cord tumours, which widen the IPD; this paper itself discusses only narrowing.
- Pedicles give clear landmarks on AP films, so the IPD is easy to take; the method is simple and relatively non-invasive, p. 232 (PDF 8).
- Main limitation: the IPD says nothing about the soft-tissue part of the canal, and soft tissue can compress the nerves too, p. 232 (PDF 8).
- Use the Table 2 limits by sex and level; outside them suspect pathology or anomaly; inside them an abnormal canal is still possible, p. 228 (PDF 4).
- Norms must match both the geographical population and the age group; younger subjects lower the means (canal distinctly narrower before 19, citing Hinck), and Eisenstein's series ran from 16 to 96 years, p. 232 (PDF 8). The author leans on regional (environmental) differences in canal size to explain Eisenstein's low values, judging the figures themselves unlikely to be wrong, p. 232 (PDF 8).
- Trisomy 21: IPD may fall from above downwards, p. 232 (PDF 8).
- The canal to body ratio frees the appraisal from the magnification factor and the person's build, p. 231 (PDF 7).
- Thick pedicles could in theory encroach on the canal, but in this series pedicle size varied without narrowing the canal, p. 230 (PDF 6).
- Racial, ethnic and regional differences make these norms of forensic interest, p. 232 (PDF 8); the summary concludes that normal canal width varies between individuals and races, p. 233 (PDF 9).
- Pitfall (Inference): comparing film values with dry-bone or CT values without the magnification factor; comparing "shortest" with "greatest" distance definitions.
- Pitfall (Inference): with white American (Hinck) norms many normal Nigerians would read as narrow; the sex- and population-specific limits must be used.

## Instructions for the autonomous anatomist

1. On an AP lumbar radiograph or AP fluoroscopy frame, find the paired pedicle rings at each lumbar level and measure the IPD as the shortest gap between their medial cortical borders, one value per vertebra (p. 226 (PDF 2), Fig. 1).
2. Do not read "maximum and minimum" as two measurements: they are the upper and lower normal limits (95% range) of the single shortest gap (question 2, p. 225 (PDF 1); Table 2 and Fig. 4, p. 229 (PDF 5)). When another source uses the greatest distance between pedicles (Eisenstein, as reported p. 232 (PDF 8)), do not pool its values with these.
3. Expect the IPD to rise from L1 to L5 with the biggest step from L4 to L5 (men +2.7 mm, women +3.0 mm; Table 1, p. 228 (PDF 4)). Inference: use the L4-L5 jump and the widest IPD as a caudal anchor; do not use the IPD to separate L1 from L2 in men (22.6 vs 22.7).
4. If the IPD falls caudally instead of rising, flag a possible dysplasia (trisomy 21 is the paper's example, p. 232 (PDF 8)); Inference: then stop using the IPD gradient as a level cue for that spine.
5. Before comparing a CT-derived IPD with Tables 1-2, remember the tables are uncorrected film values at 100 cm anode-film distance (p. 226 (PDF 2)): Inference: scale the CT value by an estimated AP magnification, or compare the canal to body ratio instead, which the author says needs no magnification factor (p. 231 (PDF 7)).
6. Measure body width at the narrowest waist of the body on the AP view (p. 226 (PDF 2)); expect it to rise steadily from L1 to L5 with the smallest step from L1 to L2 (Table 4, p. 231 (PDF 7)). Inference: never substitute an end-plate width.
7. Compute A/B; it should sit near 0.5-0.6 at every level (printed 0.6, Table 4, p. 231 (PDF 7); 0.52-0.57 from the means, Inference). Inference: a level whose A/B is far below its neighbours suggests a narrow canal at that level, not a different level name.
8. Judge an individual IPD against the sex- and level-specific Table 2 limits; outside means suspect pathology or anomaly, inside does not clear the canal (p. 228 (PDF 4)); state that the norms are for Nigerians aged 20-45 (p. 225 (PDF 1)).
9. Before using any norm table, check that it matches the person's population and age group, and whether it was measured on film or bone and as shortest or greatest distance (p. 232 (PDF 8)); under age 19 expect a narrower canal than these adult norms (citing Hinck, p. 232 (PDF 8)).
10. Measure the pedicle index on one stated side (the paper uses the right pedicle, p. 226 (PDF 2)); check the film's side marker, since Fig. 2 (p. 227 (PDF 3)) marks the ring on the viewer's right with no marker. Treat it as a weak, noisy level cue (CV 13.0-20.6%, Table 3, p. 230 (PDF 6)).
11. Inference from Tables 1 and 4: do not use the IPD to sex a spine; men exceed women by only 0.2-1.3 mm (Table 1, p. 228 (PDF 4)), while body width differs more (2.3-3.9 mm, Table 4, p. 231 (PDF 7)).
12. Remember that a normal bony IPD does not exclude stenosis from soft tissue (p. 232 (PDF 8)).
13. Do not expect any transitional-vertebra guidance from this paper; take LSTV and rib-count priors from other sources (nothing on them here, pp. 225-233).

## For bordercall's generator and classifier

(a) Anomaly priors for the sequence decoder: none. The paper gives no prevalence of LSTV, L6, T13, rib anomalies or block vertebrae, and does not say how levels were counted. Its only anomaly statement is qualitative: a caudally decreasing IPD in trisomy 21 (p. 232 (PDF 8)), which, if added, belongs as a rare "reversed canal gradient" state, not a numbering transition (Inference).

(b) Measures:
- IPD (canal width at the pedicles): the generator's canal width is the matching measure. Biases when extracting from a mask (Inference): Tables 1-2 are film values, larger than true size by the AP magnification; this paper's IPD is the minimum gap over the pedicles' height, so a mask value taken at any other single slice, or as the maximum foramen width, can only run larger; Panjabi's canal width is a dry-bone value without magnification, and whether it is taken at the same place must be checked against the Panjabi note before any pooling.
- Vertebral body waist width: not a Panjabi measure (Panjabi gives end-plate widths). Could be added as "body width at mid-height" drawn from the end-plate widths; the mask recipe is above. It is the paper's size normaliser (p. 231 (PDF 7)).
- Canal to body ratio: level-invariant (Table 4), so it has no value as a level likelihood, but it is a good check on the patient size factor and on magnification in 2D (it cancels magnification to first order) (Inference).
- Pedicle index: approximately the product of pedicle width and height in AP projection; the generator's pedicle width and height multiply to a comparable quantity only after the projection effect (pedicle convergence widens the AP ring, increasingly toward L5) and squared magnification (Inference); the paper does not report C and D separately.

(c) Measurement error: none reported. Use 0.1 mm as instrument resolution only; the SDs in Tables 1 and 3 are between-person spread, not error. Inference: when using Tables 1-2 as a 2D likelihood, add a magnification uncertainty of several percent.

(d) Covariates: sex (Tables 1-4: small for IPD, larger for body width and pedicle index); age fixed at 20-45 (no age effect measured; younger than 19 narrower per Hinck as cited, p. 232 (PDF 8)); body size through vertebral body width, with IPD proportional to body width (p. 231 (PDF 7)); population: Nigerian adults are narrower than Hinck's white Americans at every level, wider than Eisenstein's Zulus at every level, and wider than Eisenstein's white South Africans at L3-L5 in men and L2-L5 in women (Fig. 5, p. 230 (PDF 6); text p. 229 (PDF 5)). Inference: the generator's size factor should scale IPD and body width together (constant A/B), while a population offset should be treated as a shift in IPD relative to body size only where data show it, since the author's proportionality argument across populations rests on body widths measured only in Nigerians.

## Gaps and conflicts

- Text against table, sex difference: the text says the male canal was wider by no more than 1 mm at any level, p. 227 (PDF 3), but Table 1 gives 22.6 vs 21.3 mm at L1, a 1.3 mm difference (Fig. 3 shows the same gap).
- Text against table, L4 SDs: "no difference" in SD at L4, p. 228 (PDF 4), against 1.7 vs 1.6 in Table 1.
- Text against table, L5 variability: the text calls L5 strikingly the most variable level in both sexes, p. 228 (PDF 4), but in women the L5 SD (2.0) equals that at L1 and L2, the L5 CV (7.0%) is below L1-L3 (9.4, 8.9, 7.6%), and the women's Table 2 band is 8.0 mm wide at L1, L2 and L5 alike; it holds for men (SD 2.3).
- Canal to body ratio: printed 0.6 everywhere (Table 4, p. 231 (PDF 7); p. 232 (PDF 8)), but the ratio of the tabulated means is 0.52-0.55 in men and 0.56-0.57 in women (Inference, my arithmetic); the paper does not say whether it averaged individual ratios.
- Pedicle-index variability: "males showed more variability" (p. 230 (PDF 6)) holds for SD but not for CV at L2 (women 20.6% vs men 18.6%).
- Fig. 4b against Table 2: read from a 300 dpi render, the shaded female band at L1 spans about 18.3-24.5 mm, narrower than the 17.3-25.3 of Table 2 (Inference, approximate reading); the other levels and the male bands agree with Table 2 within about 0.3 mm. Use Table 2.
- Fig. 5 against the text "steady increase ... in all groups" (p. 229 (PDF 5)): Eisenstein's caucasoid men dip from about 24.0 at L2 to 23.0 at L3, and several Eisenstein segments are flat (Zulu men and women L2-L3, caucasoid women L1-L2 and L3-L4) (Inference from my graph reading).
- Comparison statements against Fig. 5 and Table 2 (Inference from my graph reading): the text says the black South African (Zulu) means clearly fell below the Nigerian lower limits (p. 230 (PDF 6)). In men the plotted Zulu means (about 21.1, 22.1, 22.0, 23.0, 26.0) lie above the Table 2 lower limits at every level (by about 0.4 to 2.8 mm); in women they lie above at L1-L3, at the limit at L4 (about 22.1 vs 22.2) and below it only at L5 (about 24.1 vs 24.4). They lie below the Nigerian means at every level, which is perhaps what was meant. - Unmeasured assumption: the author suggests that differences in physique between Hinck's sample and his own would make IPDs differ in proportion (p. 231 (PDF 7)), but neither study measured stature, and body width was measured only in the Nigerians (p. 232 (PDF 8) notes no earlier study correlated canal width with stature).

- Inference from memory: the introduction groups Eisenstein (1977) with Hinck as values "measured from plain radiographs" (p. 225 (PDF 1)), but Eisenstein's study, as I recall it (its title speaks of "morphometry", p. 233 (PDF 9)), measured dry lumbar vertebrae of 433 South African skeletons (2,166 vertebrae).
