That analysis is a Gaussian copula with an LKJ prior, joining marginals taken from anatomists' tables to correlations taken from 2,000 CTs.

**The power calculation of 28 October changes where the marginals come from.** No meta-analysis of the published series matches 2,000 CTs of the population the rule names, however many cadavers it pools. That assumes the CTs come from that population; CTs pooled from other populations carry a population term of their own (the note, part A). There are three reasons:

- Precision, at best. If every study had measured the population named, and calipers agreed with the SPINEPS chord within about 0.06 mm, about 1,700 cadavers would match 2,000 scans on each level's mean (the median over the 704 level-and-measure cells), at the CT errors measures.py assumes. The recorded series fall short even then. Panjabi's 12 spines are the only series at 675 of the 704 level-and-measure cells. The four SPINEPS measures the series reach have 22 to 144 cadavers recorded at T12 to L5, and the pedicle width, which SPINEPS does not read, about 790 at L1 to L5 as its table records them, though spine-program pull request #239 (branch claude/238) finds four of those rows' cohorts wrong.
- The studies do not agree. Panjabi's own comparison tables show published means differing between studies by a median 0.39 of a person's SD, over 86 level-and-measure cells and 14 studies, after sampling error is taken out. Over the five measures the rule reads the median is 0.50; pooled over cells the spread is 0.62 to 0.72, the 0.72 falling to 0.55 without the cervical endplate area's 6 cells, which carry 38% of its sum; with the rows the papers attribute to another definition left out the median is 0.27. At those spreads a meta-analysis of any size is worth at most about 2 to 16 scans for each level's mean (about 4 to 16 without those 6 cells), and 3 to 10 for the measures the rule reads (medians over the cells).
- Tables print no correlations.

Naming needs less than a match, and on synthetic columns the published series still fall short. A table built from them, disagreeing as Panjabi's comparison tables show, names about 2 to 13 points worse than one from 2,000 people, however many cadavers it pools. How far turns on how closely one measure follows itself from level to level within a person, and on the earlier studies' specimen counts, neither of which a table prints. At the kindest reading its 0.99 answers sit at the edge of the rule's promise at 3 bodies, right 99.06% of the time over 36 tables with 11 of them below 99%. From a correlation of 0.5 up, the table is worth about 12 to 25 people of the population named at 3 bodies and breaks the promise there; at 9 bodies it breaks at a correlation of 1 and, narrowly, at 0.8, both on the note's 4 drawn tables alone. About 50 people of that population, measured exactly, come within 2 to 4 points of 2,000 and keep it at 3 and 9 bodies on the note's 300 columns, though bordercall's build-up study, counting the columns' error, cannot tell at 3 bodies whether 50 keep it. From CTs that takes about 60 for the means and 68 for the SDs, once the CT error is taken out of each SD and the rule's size term out of the table. With the size left in, 58 CTs lose about 6 points at 3 bodies and answer half as many fields at 0.99.

**So build the table from the CTs.** Take each level's mean and SD, and the correlations, from the normal C2-countable CTs as SPINEPS measures them, with the CT error's variance taken out of each SD and the rule's size term out of the table. The CTs also give the correlation of a measure across a person's levels, which decides how far the series fall short. If the CTs come from a few other populations, their means carry a population term too, worth about as much as the prior: 5.8 to 7.3 scans per level mean for 3 to 20 populations at a spread of 0.39 (medians over the 704 cells). What they surely add then is that they are measured as the rule reads them, with their SDs and correlations. Give the series two jobs:

- a prior on the means, whose width is the spread between studies;
- a check of CT chords against caliper chords.

**What to take from the analysis.** Take its copula, and an LKJ prior on the 32-by-32 correlation between measures. Keep the structure across levels as the rule's offset loading, or as a Kronecker product. Leave out its Chamfer registration, which is segmentation that SPINEPS already does.
